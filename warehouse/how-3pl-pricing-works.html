How 3PL pricing works: every fee line, explained

Receiving, storage, pick and pack, order entry, prep. What each line on a 3PL quote pays for, what the industry typically charges, and the fees that never make it onto the first page.

Epic Warehouse Operations · November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

The short version

  • Almost every 3PL bill is built from the same five blocks: receiving, storage, fulfillment, order handling and special projects.
  • Headline per-order rates rarely tell you the real cost. Compare a full month for your actual order mix, not one line.
  • Ask every provider about minimums, setup, account management, shipping markup and how storage is measured, because that is where quotes drift apart.

If you have collected quotes from two or three fulfillment providers, you have probably noticed they don't line up. One quotes per pallet, another per cubic foot. One bundles packaging, another bills it separately. One charges for receiving by the hour. That's not an accident. Pricing structure is how 3PLs differentiate, and it makes apples-to-apples comparisons harder than they should be.

The good news is that underneath the formatting, nearly every 3PL charges for the same handful of activities. Once you know what each line pays for, you can read any quote, including ours.

1. Receiving: getting your product onto the shelf

Receiving covers unloading your inbound shipment, counting it against what you said was coming, inspecting it, and putting it away into a storage location. Providers bill it a few different ways: per pallet, per carton, per unit, per container, or per labor hour. Industry benchmarks published by Speed Commerce put typical receiving at roughly $25 to $50 per pallet or $0.30 to $1.50 per unit.1

What drives the price up is anything that makes counting slower: mixed SKUs on one pallet, loose floor-loaded cartons, missing labels, or a shipment nobody knew was coming.

How Epic bills it: $12 per single-SKU pallet, $18 per multi-SKU pallet, $5 per single-SKU box and $10 per multi-SKU box. We ask for an ASN in ShipHero before anything arrives, listing the SKUs and quantities to expect. (Here's how to prep an inbound shipment.)

2. Storage: paying for the space you occupy

Storage is usually billed monthly, by pallet position, bin or shelf, or cubic foot. Benchmarks land between $8 and $40 per pallet per month, with most providers in the $15 to $25 range.1

Two details matter as much as the rate itself:

  • How storage is measured. A monthly snapshot, an anniversary date and daily proration can produce very different bills at the same headline price.1
  • What counts as one pallet. Oversized or partial pallets are often rounded up. Know the height limit before you build your inbound pallets.
How Epic bills it: $17 a month per pallet position and $5 a month per bin or shelf location, calculated monthly and billed every two weeks. A pallet taller than 60 inches is rebuilt as two pallets and charged as two.

3. Fulfillment: pick, pack and label

This is the line most people compare first: the cost to pull an order from storage, pack it and put a shipping label on it. Most direct-to-consumer 3PLs charge a base price per order that includes the first item, plus a smaller charge for each additional unit. Speed Commerce's benchmarks put the base at about $2.50 to $3.50 per order plus $0.20 to $0.50 per extra item.1

Read the fine print on what's included. Packaging materials, void fill, inserts and the label itself are sometimes bundled and sometimes billed as separate lines.

How Epic bills it: D2C orders (Shopify, Amazon FBM and similar) are $1.70 per order with the first unit included, plus $0.30 per additional unit. That covers picking, packing, standard brown-paper void fill if needed, and the shipping label. Shipping and packaging are billed on top, unless you provide your own packaging or ship on your own carrier accounts through ShipHero.

Case and pallet (B2B) orders

Wholesale orders, retail replenishment and Amazon FBA restocks move in master cartons and pallets, not polybags. They're typically priced per case or per pallet built. At Epic a case order is $4.00, a single-SKU pallet order is $20.00, a mixed-SKU pallet is $30.00, and a wood pallet from us adds $12.00. Those rates assume no prep work, which brings us to the next line.

4. Order handling and integrations

Some providers charge a monthly platform or integration fee. Others charge per order when their staff have to key orders in manually. The lesson is the same either way: automated order flow is cheaper than email.

How Epic bills it: orders sent through the built-in ShipHero API integration, or entered by you in your own ShipHero portal, carry no entry fee. Orders we have to enter ourselves, including any order sent by email, are $2.00 each.

5. Special projects and prep

Kitting, bundling, bagging, barcode labeling, inspections and extra inventory counts are almost always billed as labor, either hourly or per unit. Ask a provider to estimate the hours for a sample project so you can compare.

How Epic bills it: $35 per hour for kitting, bagging, bundling, labeling, inventory inspection, extra cycle counts, full physical counts and any other work outside the services above. Amazon barcode labels count as prep.

The fees that don't make the first page

The gap between a 3PL's quoted rates and the all-in monthly cost can be large. Speed Commerce estimates it can reach 60 to 120 percent.1 Before you sign with anyone, ask directly about:

  • Monthly minimums, which bite hardest in slow or seasonal months.
  • Setup and onboarding fees, especially for custom integrations.
  • Account management fees for a dedicated rep.
  • Shipping markup on carrier rates, and card processing surcharges.
  • Long-term storage surcharges on slow-moving inventory.
  • Non-compliant receiving penalties for unlabeled or unannounced freight.
  • Exit terms: how long storage and minimums keep running after you give notice.

Other operators have published similar checklists, and they're worth reading side by side.2,3

How to compare quotes fairly

  1. Write down one typical month: orders, units per order, inbound pallets or cartons, pallets and bins stored, and any prep.
  2. Price that month line by line with every provider.
  3. Add shipping and packaging separately so you're comparing warehouse cost to warehouse cost.
  4. Divide by orders to get an all-in cost per order. That's the number to compare.
